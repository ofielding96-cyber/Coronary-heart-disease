Key words

Cardiac muscle
The muscle that makes up the wall of the heart.
Coronary arteries
Blood vessels on the surface of the heart that supply the cardiac muscle with oxygenated blood.
Coronary heart disease (CHD)
A disease in which fatty material builds up in the walls of the coronary arteries and narrows them.
Lumen
The space inside a blood vessel that the blood flows through.
Cholesterol
A fatty substance in the blood. Too much can lead to fatty deposits in the arteries.
Aerobic respiration
Respiration that uses oxygen to release energy from glucose.
Heart attack
Part of the heart muscle is starved of oxygen because its blood supply is blocked, so its cells are damaged or die.
Stent
A small mesh tube placed in a narrowed artery to keep it open.
Statin
A medicine that lowers blood cholesterol.
Heart valve
A flap that opens and closes so blood flows one way through the heart. Faulty valves can be replaced with mechanical or biological valves.
Anticoagulant
A drug that reduces blood clotting.
Heart failure
The heart cannot pump enough blood around the body.
Donor
A person whose healthy organ is used to replace a patient’s damaged organ.
Rejection
When the patient’s immune system attacks a transplanted organ.
Immunosuppressant
A drug that reduces the activity of the immune system to lower the risk of rejection.
Artificial heart
A mechanical device that can support or replace the pumping of the heart.

Task 2 – Coronary Heart Disease 5–15 min

Read this first

The heart is made of cardiac muscle. Like all living cells, cardiac muscle cells need a constant supply of oxygen and glucose for aerobic respiration, which releases the energy they use to contract. This blood is delivered by the coronary arteries, which branch from the aorta and run over the surface of the heart.

Coronary heart disease (CHD) happens when fatty material (including cholesterol) builds up in the walls of the coronary arteries. This narrows the lumen (the space inside the artery) and reduces the flow of blood to the heart muscle.

As a result, less oxygen reaches the cardiac muscle cells, so less aerobic respiration takes place and less energy is released. The heart cannot work as effectively. If the blood supply becomes severely restricted or blocked (for example by a blood clot), heart muscle can be damaged. This can cause a heart attack.

Task 3 – Stents and Statins 15–25 min

Read this first

Doctors can treat coronary heart disease in different ways. One way deals with the narrowed artery itself. Another lowers the cholesterol in the blood so that less fatty material builds up.

Stents are small mesh tubes inserted into a narrowed coronary artery (usually threaded in on a thin tube through a blood vessel). The stent holds the artery open, which improves blood flow to the heart muscle. Risks include blood clots and complications from the procedure.

Statins are medicines that lower blood cholesterol. This slows the rate at which fatty material builds up in the arteries and helps reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease. They are usually taken over a long period and may cause side effects.

Task 4 – Other Heart Treatments 25–35 min

Read this first

Replacement heart valves. Heart valves keep blood flowing one way. A valve can become faulty: it may not open fully, or it may leak so that blood flows backwards. A faulty valve can be replaced with a mechanical or a biological valve. Mechanical valves can last a long time but patients usually need lifelong anticoagulant medication (to reduce blood clotting). Biological valves may deteriorate and need replacing.

Heart transplants. In heart failure (when the heart cannot pump enough blood), a severely damaged heart can sometimes be replaced with a donor heart. There is a shortage of suitable donors, and patients need immunosuppressant drugs to reduce the risk of the immune system rejecting the new heart.

Artificial hearts. Mechanical devices can support or replace the pumping of the heart. They may be used while a patient waits for a transplant, or as longer-term support. Risks include blood clots, infection and mechanical failure.
